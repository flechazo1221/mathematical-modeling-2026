12        Abstract

13   The structural heterogeneities of fruits and vegetables intensify the complexity to comprehend

14   the interrelated physicochemical changes that occur during drying. Shrinkage of food materials

15   during drying is a common physical phenomenon which affects the textural quality and taste

16   of the dried product. The shrinkage of food material depends on many factors including

17   material characteristics, microstructure, mechanical properties, and process conditions.

18   Understanding the effect of these influencing factors on deformation of fruits and vegetables

19   during drying is crucial to obtain better-quality product. The majority of the previous studies

20   regarding shrinkage are either experimental or empirical; however, such studies cannot provide

21   a realistic understanding of the physical phenomena behind the material shrinkage. In contrast,

22   theoretical modelling can provide better insights into the shrinkage that accompanies

23   simultaneous heat and mass transfer during drying. However, limited studies have been

24   conducted on the theoretical modelling of shrinkage of fruits and vegetables. Therefore, the

25   main aim of this paper is to critically review the existing theoretical shrinkage models and

26   present a framework for a theoretical model for the shrinkage mechanism. This paper also

                                                       1

[[PAGE 3]]
27   describes the effect of different drying conditions on material shrinkage. Discussions on how

28   the diverse characteristics of fruits and vegetables affect shrinkage propagation is presented.

29   Moreover, a comprehensive review of formulation techniques of shrinking models and their

30   results are also presented. Finally, the challenges in developing a physics-based shrinkage

31   model are discussed.

81   1. Introduction

 82          Fruits and vegetables are heterogeneous in structure and are comprised of about 80-

 83   90% water which is contained in different cellular environments. This vast amount of water

 84   makes these materials highly perishable and therefore an appropriate food preservation

 85   technique is essential (Karim and Hawlader, 2005; Khan, Joardder, Kumar, & Karim, 2016b).

 86   Drying is one of the most effective food preserving methods that can be applied to these

 87   perishable food materials. The porous and hygroscopic nature of fruits and vegetables make it

 88   highly shrinkable while drying. Transport of water from cellular locations to the surrounding

 89   environment causes irregular volume changes of high-moisture foods during drying, and this

 90   volume reduction is usually defined as a material shrinkage (Khan & Karim, 2017a). The

 91   shrinkage of fruits and vegetables is sometimes referred as deformation of material, and it is

 92   an obvious physical phenomenon commonly observed during drying.

 93          Shrinkage of dried products has many negative consequences, ranging from quality to

 94   consumer satisfaction (Mulet, Garcia-Reverter, Bon, & Berna, 2000; Ochoa, Kesseler, Pirone,

 95   Márquez, & Michelis, 2002; Mayor & Sereno, 2004; Udomkun and Nagle et al. 2016), and

 96   including some other adverse effects, such as surface cracking and reduction of rehydration

 97   capability (Jayaraman, Gupta, & Rao, 1990; Senadeera, Bhandari, Young, & Wijesinghe,

 98   2005). Shrinkage has a great effect on mechanical and textural properties of fruits and

 99   vegetables. For example, Vincent (1989) found that the torsional stiffness (0.5–7 MPa) of the

100   apple samples varied with the shrinkage. Moreover, shrinkage is an important factor that

101   enormously affects the drying rate as well as drying kinetics. Food researchers have argued

102   that shrinkage should not be neglected while predicting actual heat and mass transfer during

103   drying (Aprajeeta, Gopirajah, & Anandharamakrishnan, 2015). A model with shrinkage fits

104   better with experimental data during drying than a model without shrinkage (Aprajeeta et al.,

105   2015; Pacheco-Aguirre et al., 2015). Thus numerous studies for investigating material

                                                    4

[[PAGE 6]]
106   shrinkage have been conducted. Most of the research has been based on extensive experimental

107   analysis (Madamba, Driscoll, & Buckle, 1994; Krokida, Zogzas, & Maroulis, 1997; Krokida,

108   Oreopoulou, & Maroulis, 2000; Yan, Gallagher, & Oliveira, 2008; Dissa & Desmorieux et al.

109   2010; Mercier & Villeneuve et al. 2011; Liu & Chen et al. 2012; Madiouli & Sghaier et al.

110   2012; Koua, Koffi, & Gbaha, 2017).

111          Experimental studies have very limited applicability because they are conducted under

112   highly specific product and process conditions (Zogzas, Maroulis, & Marinos, 1994; Rahman

113   & Perera, et al. 1996; Krokida & Maroulis, 1997; Krokida, Karathanos, & Maroulis, 1998;

114   McLaughlin & Magee, 1998). Mathematical modeling can provide better insight into the

115   causes of shrinkage with continuous heat and mass transfer during drying. As a consequence,

116   many mathematical models have been developed for material shrinkage. They can be broadly

117   categorized as empirical models, semi-empirical models, and theoretical models.

118          To uncover the physical phenomena of shrinkage, many empirical models have been

119   formulated based on experimental data that are suitable for a specified product and processing

120   conditions (Tsukada, Sakai, & Hayakawa, 1991; Lang & Sokhansanj, 1993; Ratti, 1994; Izumi

121   & Hayakawa, 1995; Rovedo, Suarez, & Viollaz, 1995; Akiyama, Liu, & Hayakawa, 1997;

122   McMinn & Magee, 1997a; Rovedo, Suárez, & Viollaz, 1997; Gabas, Menegalli, & Telis-

123   Romero, 1999; ; Vázquez, Chenlo, Moreira, & Costoyas, 1999). In response to the limitations

124   of empirical models, a slightly improved model has been formulated that can generate a

125   theoretical understanding (Kilpatrick, Lowe, & Arsdel, 1955; Suzuki & Kubota et al. 1976;

126   Lozano, Rotstein, & Urbicain, 1980, 1983; Perez & Calvelo, 1984; Rahman et al. 1996).

127   Nevertheless, the oversimplified assumptions of these types of models prevent them from

128   providing a realistic understanding of the fundamental causes of shrinkage during drying. In

129   contrast, theoretical models are developed considering fundamental physics and therefore they

                                                    5

[[PAGE 7]]
130   can predict the physicochemical changes accurately during dehydration of fruits and

131   vegetables.

132           The complex structure of fruits and vegetables makes it very difficult to formulate a

133   mechanistic shrinkage model. To develop a physics-based model, four different models can be

134   applied for predicting shrinkage during drying of fruits and vegetables: linear elastic, hyper-

135   elastic, elastoplastic, and viscoelastic material models. These models depend on the

136   characteristics of food materials, and thus material characterization is crucial to develop a

137   shrinkage model. Moreover, an appropriate selection of these models for a specific food

138   material is vital for developing a realistic shrinkage model. In the existing literature, theoretical

139   models have been developed based on some simplified assumptions; for example, treating food

140   materials as rubbery (Gulati & Datta, 2015) or elastic (Jomaa & Puiggali, 1991; Mrani, Fras,

141   & Benet, 1995; Kowalski, 1996; Mrani & Bénet et al. 1997; Ponsart & Vasseur et al. 2003;

142   Chemkhi, Zagrouba, & Bellagi, 2004; Niamnuy & Devahastin et al. 2008). These assumptions

143   simplify the problem formulation, but they are not conducive to a realistic understanding.

144   Therefore, more rigorous study are essential to formulate a physics-based shrinkage model for

145   food drying. The main aims of this paper are to present a comprehensive review of existing

146   theoretical shrinkage models, considering the effects of the diverse nature of fruits and

147   vegetables on material shrinkage, examine how process conditions affect material shrinkage,

148   uncover current research gaps, and identify the challenges in developing a realistic physics-

149   based shrinkage model.

150   2. Characteristics of food materials

151           Food materials, particularly fruits and vegetables are heterogeneous in structure and

152   diverse in nature and therefore characterization of food materials is difficult. Fruits and

153   vegetables are mainly categorized as elastic, hyperelastic, elastoplastic, and viscoelastic which

154   are discussed below.

                                                       6

[[PAGE 8]]
155      2.1 Food material as an elastic and hyperelastic material

156          In many cases, fresh produce are considered to be elastic in nature, where the

157   deformation of material is considered to be very small (Kowalski, 1996; Mrani et al., 1997;

158   Ponsart et al., 2003; Chemkhi et al., 2004; Niamnuy et al., 2008). This elastic property strongly

159   affects the thermal stresses that are induced by the continuous penetration of heat energy. The

160   induced stresses will disappear due to the elastic deformations (Kowalski & Rybicki, 2007). In

161   practice, however, some residual stresses remain inside the material after drying which may

162   have a substantial influence on further behavior of the material during rehydration (Lewicki,

163   Rajchert, & Mariak, 1997). Furthermore, elastic behavior mainly depends on the nature of the

164   stress-strain properties of materials. Based on these properties, elastic materials can be

165   categorized as linear elastic, where Hooke’s constitutive law can be used to model the material

166   shrinkage, and nonlinear elastic (or hyperelastic), where Neo-Hookean constitutive law can be

167   used to model the material shrinkage. Many models that consider fruits and vegetables as linear

168   elastic have been developed for shrinkage during drying (Jomaa & Puiggali, 1991; Mrani et

169   al., 1995; Kowalski, 1996; Mrani et al., 1997; Ponsart et al., 2003; Chemkhi et al., 2004;

170   Niamnuy et al., 2008). According to Hooke’s constitutive law, the stress and strain remain

171   proportional up to a certain limit (about 10%) that is not defined for diverse fruits and

172   vegetables. Therefore, consideration of fruits and vegetables as a linear elastic material may

173   only be applied to certain type of products and not be an effective approach due to the large

174   deformation that occurs during drying and generating strains over the Hookean range (Gulati

175   & Datta, 2015). Instead, a nonlinear elastic model may better predict large deformations of

176   fruits and vegetables during drying. Considering the benefits of nonlinear elastic properties,

177   some researchers have used Neo-Hookean constitutive law for developing a realistic shrinkage

178   model (Dhall & Datta, 2011; Gulati & Datta, 2015). They developed their model based on a

179   poromechanics concept for a less porous material (potato); however, it may be difficult to apply

                                                     7

[[PAGE 9]]
180   their model to other highly porous material such as apple and eggplant tissues because the

181   porosity of potato is much lower than that of apple and eggplant.

182      2.2 Food material as an elastoplastic material

183          Fruits and vegetables are sometimes defined as an elastoplastic material when it

184   exhibits both elastic and plastic properties i.e. a rubberlike plastic. It depends on the state of

185   stress between the elastic limit and the breaking strength. It is assumed that food material shows

186   the elastoplastic properties in a small strain region (Tsukada et al., 1991; Izumi & Hayakawa,

187   1995; Akiyama & Hayakawa, 2000; Yang, Sakai, & Watanabe, 2001; Curcio & Aversa, 2014),

188   while others have argued that the deformation of fresh produce are significant (about 30-70%)

189   (Gulati & Datta, 2015). Moreover, within a small strain region the potato behavior has been

190   assumed as elastoplastic and non-isotropic (Yang et al., 2001), while others have postulated

191   that this assumption is not always suitable for other types of vegetables (Llave et al., 2016).

192   This is mainly because of the lack of material characterization. Predicting material shrinkage

193   without a material characterization, it is obviously not a justified inception due to the diverge

194   nature of fruits and vegetables. In this context, viscoelastic properties might be the better

195   assumption for characterizing fresh produce, are discussed below.

196      2.3 Food material as a viscoelastic material

197          Viscoelasticity is the property of materials that exhibit both elastic and viscous

198   characteristics when undergoing deformation. The stress-strain relationship of those types of

199   materials depends on time. This type of material shows strain rate dependency during

200   compression, creep, stress relaxation, and very often hysteresis. Most of the plant-based food

201   materials exhibit those types of properties that lead to consideration of the high-moisture foods

202   as viscoelastic (Mahiuddin, Khan, Pham, & Karim, 2018). During food drying, the mechanical

203   behavior of viscoelastic food material significantly affects the process conditions as well as

                                                      8

[[PAGE 10]]
204   sensory properties (Lu, Ma, Wang, & Yu, 2015). Many experimental and theoretical studies

205   have been conducted to reflect the influence of various drying parameters on viscoelastic

206   materials and to accurately describe the stress-strain relationship of food materials (Lu et al.,

207   2015). These studies have used basic constitutive Maxwell and Kelvin models to predict stress-

208   strain relationship for food materials. Maxwell and Kelvin models have been selected as the

209   best approach to predict the shrinkage of food materials because fruits and vegetables are

210   composed of a mixture of solid and liquid which exhibits the characteristics of a spring and

211   dashpot.

212   3. Mechanism of shrinkage

213          Structural heterogeneity makes it complex to understand the physicochemical changes

214   that occur in fruits and vegetables during thermal processing. During food drying,

215   microstructural stress is induced by the moisture and temperature gradient within the product.

216   This microstructural stress leads to material deformation. This deformation can be referred to

217   as shrinkage of the material. In most food processing, there are two main causes of material

218   shrinkage. Firstly, the food tissue is incapable of holding its structural arrangement when the

219   space occupied by water is constantly emptied and air-filled (Khan et al., 2016b). Secondly,

220   the exterior skin structure collapse leads to shrinkage (Panyawong & Devahastin, 2007).

221   Besides these, the cell shape remains intact due to the turgor pressure within individual cells

222   (Rahman, Joardder, Khan, Nghia, & Karim, 2016). Turgor pressure is a fluid force inside the

223   cell of high-moisture foods that pushes against the cell wall to maintain the cell wall’s rigidity.

224   If the turgor pressure fails during drying, the cellular structure will collapse due to internal

225   thermal stresses (Lin & Pitt, 1986; Prothon, Ahrne, & Sjoholm, 2003). The changes of the

226   turgor pressure in potato tissue cause changes in the tissue compressive strength, the critical

227   strain, and also the critical stress that leads to cell collapse (Joardder, Karim, Brown, & Kumar,

228   2015b). Therefore, shrinkage of material not only depends on moisture content but also

                                                      9

[[PAGE 11]]
229   depends on cell wall rigidity and turgor pressure of intracellular water, as well as cell wall

230   properties that are discussed below.

231   4. Influencing factors that affect material shrinkage during drying

232          Due to the diverse nature of fruits and vegetables, there are many factors that can affect

233   the magnitude of material shrinkage during drying. Cellular structure, mechanical properties

234   of food material, and drying conditions are the predominant factors that strongly affect the

235   magnitude of shrinkage, and they are discussed below.

236      4.1 Effect of cellular water transport on material shrinkage

237          Fruits and vegetables are mostly composed of a solid matrix with a significant amount

238   of liquid water in the cell at different level. The structural rigidity of cellular tissue prevents

239   shrinkage when subjected to drying process (Prothon et al., 2003). The structural rigidity

240   depends on cellular water distribution and its characteristics. Fruits and vegetables are

241   hygroscopic in nature and contain 80-90% water (Khan, Kumar, Joardder, & Karim, 2017d).

242   This vast amount of water is located in different cellular environments such as the intracellular

243   environment, intercellular environment, and the cell wall environment. The proportion of water

244   that present inside the cell (intracellular spaces) is referred to as intracellular water, and water

245   that is present in intercellular spaces is referred to as intercellular water or capillary water

246   (Khan, Wellard, Nagy, Joardder, & Karim, 2016b).

247          The cell wall is mostly composed of solid material, but a very small amount of water is

248   present in microcapillaries. This water is referred to as cell wall water. Migration of free water

249   has little effect on material shrinkage; however, transport of intracellular and cell wall water

250   strongly affects the material shrinkage during food processing (Joardder et al., 2015a). During

251   drying, transport of intracellular water causes cellular shrinkage, pore formation, and collapse

                                                      10

[[PAGE 12]]
252   of the cell. Finally, overall food tissue is deformed due to the migration of cell wall water

253   (Joardder et al., 2015b).

254          Very recently, Khan, et al. (2017b, 2018) argued that intracellular water migrates from

255   the intracellular region to the intercellular environment through rupturing the cell membrane.

256   They proposed that cell membranes collapse progressively at different stages of drying rather

257   than collapsing at once, while others argued that cell membrane of food tissue collapsed at a

258   time that ultimately deformed the whole tissue at a certain drying period (Halder, Datta, &

259   Spanswick, 2011).

260          Migration of cellular water affects the cellular shrinkage but the magnitude of shrinkage

261   is still not clearly understood. Furthermore, cell walls are basic construction elements that are

262   responsible for the strength of the whole tissue (Haman & Konstankiewicz, 2000). The cell

263   wall thickness of various types of fruits and vegetables depends on the characteristics of its

264   solid material content (Khan et al., 2016a). For example, cucumber tissue is more shrinkable

265   than potato because potato cell walls are thicker and stronger (Joardder, Brown, Kumar, &

266   Karim, 2015a).

267      4.2 Effect of drying conditions on material shrinkage

268      The most important drying parameters that influence the shrinkage of material are the

269   drying temperature, drying air velocity, and the relative humidity of drying air. They are

270   discussed below.

271          4.2.1     Drying air temperature

272          Drying temperature plays a significant role for increasing the drying rate that ultimately

273   affects the material shrinkage. Drying below 50°C, the cells remain intact (Halder et al., 2011);

274   therefore, conversion of the intracellular water to free water remains unchanged with the drying

275   progress. The intracellular water moves to intercellular space only through microcapillaries and

                                                     11

[[PAGE 13]]
276   therefore this migration is very slow, resulting in very low deformation. Halder et al. (2011)

277   argued that the membrane of the cells collapse at once after reaching a specific temperature

278   during processing; however, their argument may not be justified because cell collapse depends

279   on internal thermal stress (Joardder et al. 2015a) that first develops near the surface and

280   gradually penetrates to the center of the sample during convective drying (Khan & Karim,

281   2017a; Khan et al., 2017b). As a result, the cells may collapse progressively from the surface

282   to center; therefore, an anisotropic shrinkage may be observed (Prothon et al., 2003; Khan et

283   al., 2016a).

284           Furthermore, to investigate the effect of temperature on material shrinkage during

285   drying, much observation has shown that temperature has a significant effect on material

286   shrinkage during drying (Valle, Cuadros, & Aguilera, 1998). Most of the fruits and vegetables

287   experience huge volume reduction when they are subjected to drying process at low

288   temperature (20°C). In contrast, when subjected to drying at high temperature (50°C–70°C),

289   food material undergoes limited shrinkage. Likewise, Wang and Brennan (1995) found that

290   shrinkage of potato tissue at higher temperature (70°C) is lower than shrinkage at low

291   temperature (40°C). This is may be due to the case-hardening effect that controls the transport

292   of moisture, which ultimately controls material shrinkage. At low temperature, the moisture is

293   transported in a flat pattern, therefore the stresses inside the food are minimal. Consequently,

294   the material shrinkage is uniform and pronounced. On the other hand, at high temperature the

295   surface of samples is dried very quickly, causing the surface to become stiff (case hardening),

296   which does not allow the material to shrink significantly. Finally, the drying air temperature is

297   the principal controlling factor for material shrinkage, and therefore a linear correlation

298   between moisture content and air temperature is crucial (McMinn & Magee, 1997a, 1997b).

                                                     12

[[PAGE 14]]
299          4.2.2 Drying air velocity and relative humidity

300          Air velocity is another significant factor that affects material shrinkage during drying.

301   Ratti (1994) found substantial changes of shrinkage with varying air velocity. Shrinkage of

302   fruits and vegetables are decreased during drying with increasing air velocity (Ratti, 1994;

303   Khraisheh, McMinn, & Magee, 2004). This is may be due to the changes of mass transfer from

304   internal to external at different stages of drying. During drying with externally controlled

305   energy transfer, mass transfer occurs by both the internal diffusion and the external convection.

306   At low air velocities surface resistance prevails and therefore the moisture profiles in the

307   sample are relatively flat resulting in low internal stresses. Consequently, food material shrinks

308   uniformly at low air velocities. Besides this, changes in shrinkage with air velocity are not

309   equally significant for all types of fruits and vegetables; for example, the effect of velocity on

310   material shrinkage is most pronounced in potato tissues, less pronounced in apples, and

311   practically negligible in carrots (Ratti, 1994).

312          Sometimes the relative humidity of air may control the material shrinkage. At low

313   relative humidity the Biot number (a dimensionless quantity used in heat transfer calculation)

314   increases, ultimately limiting the material shrinkage (Ratti, 1994). Case hardening strongly

315   affects the amount of material shrinkage evident at low relative air humidity.

316      4.3 Effect of mechanical properties of food material on shrinkage

317          Porosity is the main driving factor that can influence material shrinkage significantly.

318   When the volume reduction of the food material is exactly equal to the volume of the removed

319   water during drying, it is known as ideal shrinkage. If this occurs, no pore formation can be

320   considered in the product. In reality, no food materials follow ideal shrinkage during drying:

321   porosity increases in food materials. Thus the porosity can be calculated from the experimental

322   shrinkage curve, an ideal shrinkage biased curve and an ideal shrinkage curve (Krokida &

                                                         13

[[PAGE 15]]
323   Maroulis, 1997). From the literature, it is found that porosity is inversely proportional to

324   material shrinkage. Katekawa and Silva (2004) have proposed a relationship between shrinkage

325   and porosity, including variables such as initial density of the wet product, true density of the

326   liquid phase, and true density of the solid phase.

327      The existing constitutive models depend on the modulus of elasticity and Poisson’s ratio.

328   The value of these two mechanical properties significantly changes throughout the drying

329   process that leads to change in the material deformation. Increasing the modulus of elasticity

330   with decreasing moisture content strongly depends on temperature. During drying, material

331   become rubbery with various degree of softness at higher temperatures during drying. Gulati

332   and Datta (2015) argued that with an increase in elastic modulus, the shear modulus of the

333   material increases leading to an increase in the principal tensile stress inside the material. In

334   addition, Poisson’s ratio is responsible for the rate of evaporation of liquid water in food

335   material. A lower Poisson’s ratio means a higher evaporation rate, which ultimately increases

336   the volumetric changes (Gulati & Datta, 2015).

337      4.4 Effect of glass transition temperature on shrinkage

338          The structure of fruits and vegetables are very complex where amorphous form of water

339   present inside the solid food matrix (Sappati, Nayak, & Walsum, 2017). The temperature at (or

340   above) which the amorphous water in the fruits and vegetables changes from a rubbery to

341   glassy state is called glass transition temperature (Tg) (Champion, Le, & Simatos, 2000). Glass

342   transition temperature is also known as solid mobility temperature. The glass transition theory

343   can explain the process of shrinkage during drying (Karathanos, Kanellopoulos, & Belessiotis,

344   1996; Krokida et al., 1998; Cnossen & Siebenmorgen, 2000; Rahman, 2001; Karathanos,

345   2007). In the rubbery state, the food material has a high mobility within the solid matrix.

346   Conversely, in the glassy state, the food material has a low mobility due to high viscosity (in

                                                     14

[[PAGE 16]]
347   the range of 1012-1013 Pa-s) (Joardder et al., 2017). The food material stays in the rubbery state

348   when its temperature is more than the glass transition temperature. The shrinkage rate is also

349   higher in the rubbery state as the molecular movement is much higher than that in the glassy

350   state. The shrinkage in the rubbery state is directly proportional to the moisture loss. The glass

351   transition temperature (Tg) varies with respect to the components types and the water content

352   in the food. When drying progresses, the glass transition temperature increases with the

353   reduction of moisture content. This phenomenon leads to lower shrinkage rate in the glassy

354   state due to high viscosity of the food material (Sappati et al., 2017). Glassy state during drying

355   usually provides the crispy texture of dried foods.

356   5. Theoretical shrinkage models

357      Fruits and vegetables are highly heterogeneous materials with solid and semisolid

358   structures that contain a three-dimensional solid network or matrix usually holding large

359   quantities of aqueous solution. The solid matrix consists mainly of biopolymer elements. In

360   more complex cases, fruits and vegetables are sometimes considered as composite material

361   which is made of additional structural elements with biopolymers. (Aguilera, Bustos, &

362   Molina, 1992). Moreover, there are many factors that affect the magnitude of shrinkage such

363   as volume of removed water, mobility of the solid matrix, drying rate, and processing

364   conditions. The diverse characteristics of high-moisture foods and process conditions make it

365   complex to understand the physics behind the material shrinkage during drying. In this context,

366   a theoretical shrinkage model could be the best option to predict the actual mechanism of

367   deformation of fruits and vegetables during drying. There are some theoretical shrinkage

368   models based on continuum mechanics which are discussed below, and also shown in Table 1.

                                                      15

[[PAGE 17]]
369         5.1 Linear elastic model

370             The linear elastic model is the basic mechanistic model of shrinkage prediction. It is

371   the most simplified model in food drying. The mechanical behavior of materials is modeled as

372   a continuous mass rather than as discrete particles. The fundamental linearizing assumptions

373   of linear elasticity are: infinitesimal strains or small deformations (or strains) and linear

374   relationships between the components of stress and strain (Kowalski, 1996; Gulati & Datta,

375   2015). In addition, linear elasticity is valid only for stress states that cannot produce yielding.

376   For linear elastic materials, Hooke’s law is used as the constitutive law.

377             Kowalski (1996) presented a basic mathematical linear elastic model describing the

378   shrinkage phenomenon of materials undergoing dehydration processes. The model was

379   constructed based on the methods of continuum mechanics and the principles of

380   thermodynamics of irreversible processes. In his model, Kowalski (1996) assumed that the

381   material is deformed simultaneously due to the progressive change of heat and moisture as well

382   as the induced stresses during drying. According to the model, the strain tensor  ij is expressed

383   as:

461      5.3 Hyperelastic model

462          A hyperelastic material model is a type of constitutive model for ideally nonlinear

463   elastic material. In this model, the stress–strain relationship derives from a strain energy density

464   function, whose stress–strain relationship can be defined as nonlinearly elastic, isotropic,

465   incompressible and generally independent of strain rate. Dhall and Datta (2011) developed a

466   poromechanics based modeling framework to describe shrinkage in fruits and vegetables. They

467   discussed two special cases of deformation such as small and large deformation of the solid

468   matrix. In their analysis, they have considered the biological materials as rubber and polymer

469   like materials which often exhibit nonlinear stress-strain behavior. This is due to the continuous

470   moisture loss and change of mechanical properties during drying, which causes the food tissue

516      5.4 Viscoelastic model

517            Viscoelasticity is the property of materials that exhibit both elastic and viscous

518   characteristics when undergoing deformation. The viscosity of a viscoelastic substance is strain

519   rate dependent. Purely elastic materials do not dissipate energy (as heat) when a load is applied,

520   then removed. However, a viscoelastic substance loses energy when a load is applied then

521   removed. Hysteresis can be observed in the stress–strain curve, with the area of the loop being

522   equal to the energy lost during the loading cycle. Viscoelasticity is studied using dynamic

523   mechanical analysis by applying a small oscillatory stress and measuring the resulting strain.

524   Itaya, Kobayashi, and Hayakawa (1995) developed a three-dimensional model of heat and mass

525   transfer for a composite body undergoing a dehydration process where they first assumed the

526   materials (hydrates of high amylose starch granules) as viscoelastic. Considering the same

527   assumptions, Sakai, Yang, and Watanabe (2002) developed a viscoelastic model to describe

                                                           24

[[PAGE 26]]
528   deformation for food materials accompanying changes in moisture content. They showed that

529   the assumption of potato is viscoelastic which is theoretically more accurate for non-uniform

530   volume reduction than the assumption of elastoplastic material during drying. To develop a

531   theoretical model for roasting of eggplant, Llave et al. (2016) assumed vegetables as

532   viscoelastic material where the constitutive relationship of stress–strain deviates from Hooke’s

533   law. However, they used a Burgers model for the constitutive relationship between stress and

534   strain, whereas Sakai et al. (2002) used a Maxwell model. A Maxwell viscoelastic model

535   cannot predict creep accurately for diverse biological materials (Mahiuddin et al. 2018).

553   6. General trends of output parameters of shrinkage

554          Based on the extensive review of the literatures on shrinkage models and the authors’

555   current research, the general trends of the different output parameters are identified and

556   presented in the following sections.

557      6.1 Change of volume with drying air velocity

558          Figure 1 shows general trends of the effect of drying air velocity on material shrinkage

559   of fruits and vegetables during drying. The rate of volume changes (V/V0, where V is the volume

560   at any time of drying, and V0 is the initial volume of the sample) at the early stage of drying is

561   not so significant. In the next stage (the middle stage) of drying, the rate of material shrinkage

562   increases significantly, followed by the ideal shrinkage. However, the rate of volume reduction

563   attenuates at the final stage of drying (Figure 1). This variation of volume reduction is governed

564   by the rate of internal mass transfer as well as process conditions. At the early stages of drying,

565   mostly free water is migrated from intercellular spaces to the environment through evaporation

566   which has minor effect on material shrinkage (Joardder et al., 2015a; Khan, Kumar, & Karim,

567   2017d; Khan et al., 2017b). After the early stage of drying, intracellular water starts to migrate

568   through rupturing the cell membrane (Khan et al., 2017b), and therefore a significant volume

569   reduction follows (Joardder et al., 2015b). After the middle stage of drying, most of the

570   intracellular water has been transferred to the intercellular spaces followed by transfer to

571   environment through evaporation. After the middle stage of drying, a small amount of water

572   remains in the micropores inside the intracellular spaces and the cell wall environment. This

573   type of water is termed as strongly bound water which is strongly bonded with the internal

574   micromolecular species and therefore difficult to transport during drying (Khan et al., 2016b;

575   Khan, Wellard, Mahiuddin, & Karim, 2017c; Khan et al., 2017e). At the final stage of drying,

576   this type of water is transported with a longer drying time that leads to attenuation of the rate

577   of volume reduction. In addition, it can be seen from Figure 1 that the volume reduction

                                                      27

[[PAGE 29]]
578   decreases with increasing drying air velocity. This is mainly due to the case hardening that

579   occurs while faster drying is imposed (as discussed in section 4.2.2).

583      6.2 Change of volume with temperature

584          During drying, volume reduction may coincide with the ideal shrinkage for much of the

585   drying process (i.e., the middle stage) but not for the initial and final stages of drying, as shown

586   in Figure 2, since shrinkage is referred to as ideal when volume change is equal to the amount

587   of liquid water lost. The volume reduction at the early stage and final stage of drying is slower

588   than the middle stage of drying. This is mainly due to the transport characteristics of different

589   types of water (free water, loosely bound water, and strongly bound water) at different stages

590   of drying (Khan et al., 2017b), as discussed in section 6.1. In addition, when drying at high

591   temperature, the surface of the material dries out much faster than the core; therefore, crust

592   formation occurs at the surface that leads to decrease the volume reduction. On the other hand,

593   at low drying temperature, the moisture is transported in a flat pattern; therefore, the stresses

594   inside the food are minimal. Consequently, there is a uniform, large material shrinkage (as

595   discussed in section 4.2.1). Moreover, at high temperature when the drying rate is higher, the

596   surface of the sample enters a glassy state while the core is still wet and in a rubbery state,

597   leading to the coexistence of both rubbery and glassy states at higher than average moisture

598   content. This rubbery–glassy phase transition is accompanied by large changes in the

599   mechanical properties and transport properties (Gulati & Datta, 2015).

601      6.3 Changes in stresses during drying

602           The principal stress (i.e., thermal stress) is induced while drying is in progress due to

603   the moisture and temperature gradient. This stress increases at different stages of drying. It

604   mainly depends on the penetration rate of heat energy during drying. Figure 3 shows the

605   variation of maximum principal tensile stress with moisture ratio. It can be seen that the stress

606   increases with the propagation of heat energy. The cell membrane of the material collapsed

607   when this stress just crosses the fracture stress line (Figure 3). Gulati and Datta (2015), found

608   that while drying at the highest rate, the principal tensile stress curve crosses the fracture stress

609   line at a specific drying time (about 1.2 hours). Likewise, Wang and Brennan (1995), reported

610   that potato samples cracked near the top surface about after one hour in the drying process. For

611   intermediate drying rates, the maximum principal tensile stress value stays below the fracture

612   stress curve, indicating that there is no material fracture. Moreover, it is also noted by Wang &

613   Brennan (1995), that there was no visible crack on the material surface during drying, and the

614   material shrank to a solid core. For the lowest drying rate, the maximum principal tensile stress

615   stays well below the fracture strength of the material and there is no crack incipience (Gulati

616   & Datta, 2015).

620   7. Challenges for prediction of material shrinkage during drying

621           Based on an extensive literature review, the following key factors contribute to the

622   current challenges to developing an accurate physics-based shrinkage model for food drying.

                                                       29

[[PAGE 31]]
623      7.1 Food material characterization

624          Due to the diverse nature of fruits and vegetables, it is very important to characterize

625   the material as to whether it is elastic, plastic, viscoelastic, elastoplastic or something else.

626   Existing research has been conducted based on some simplistic assumptions regarding the

627   behavior of fruits and vegetables, including that it behaves like elastic (i.e., a rubberlike

628   material) (Dhall & Datta, 2011; Gulati & Datta, 2015; Gulati et al., 2016), elastoplastic

629   (Tsukada et al., 1991; Izumi & Hayakawa, 1995; Akiyama & Hayakawa, 2000; Yang et al.,

630   2001; Curcio & Aversa, 2014), and viscoelastic (Itaya et al., 1995; Perré & May, 2001; Sakai

631   et al., 2002; Lu et al., 2015; Llave et al., 2016; ). However, the heterogeneous structure of

632   different fruits and vegetables makes its actual behavior complex, and therefore, without

633   material characterization, categorization of the material behavior may not be justified. By

634   interpreting the necessity of material characterization, much research has been done on the

635   characterization of different porous materials, such as for polymers (Paola, Pirrotta, & Valenza,

636   2011). Nevertheless, there is no study that adequately addresses the categorization of the

637   behavior of fruits and vegetables. To investigate some of the mechanical properties, a few

638   researchers have conducted experimental analysis of stress relaxation behavior (Lu & Puri,

639   1992; Blahovec, 1996; Lewicki & Jakubczyk, 2004; Roopa & Bhattacharya, 2014). Although

640   results of those studies are significant for calculating material properties, material

641   characterization is still lacking. This is because of the complexity of stress–strain relaxation

642   analysis of diverse food materials.

643      7.2 Modelling of shrinkage of food materials

644          Existing models are based on some simplistic assumptions to minimize the complexity

645   of the model formulation and calculation. In most cases, it is considered that fruits and

646   vegetables are deformed on a small scale and therefore they have used Hooke’s law for up to

                                                     30

[[PAGE 32]]
647   10% strain levels during drying. However, these assumptions may not true for different types

648   of food materials because most of the fruits and vegetables undergo a large anisotropic

649   deformation when they are subjected to simultaneous heat and mass transfer processes during

650   drying (Dhall & Datta, 2011; Gulati & Datta, 2015). These types of simplistic assumptions

651   have been used in the existing literature in order to reduce the complexity of the model

652   formulation and solution. Therefore, for accurate prediction of material shrinkage a realistic

653   generalized theoretical model is needed and that is the challenge for further research.

654          In addition, the classical viscoelastic material models that have been used in the existing

655   literature have some limitations. For instance, to reproduce the actual material’s viscoelastic

656   behavior (with creep and relaxation phases) many parameters are needed which makes the

657   model more complex. Moreover, the parameters are found by means of best fitting numerical

658   procedures which can lead to meaningless parameters from a physical point of view (e.g.,

659   negative coefficients of stiffness and viscosity in a spring and dashpot) (Paola et al., 2011).

660   Besides this, most of the current literature has used the classical Maxwell model; however, this

661   model cannot accurately predict the creep property of the viscoelastic fruits and vegetables.

662      7.3 Mechanical properties of food materials

663          Shrinkage of fruits and vegetables strongly depends on the mechanical properties. Most

664   of the existing literature considered the modulus of elasticity of potato tissue as a function of

665   moisture content; however, the modulus of elasticity also depends on the temperature and the

666   material structure (Khan et al., 2016a). Moreover, the modulus of elasticity relationship that

667   has been widely used in the current literature was based on data from experiments conducted

668   at room temperature (Yang et al., 2001). Instead, for a realistic understanding, an accurate

669   relationship of modulus of elasticity is needed based on data that has been generated while

670   drying is in progress; and this is the ultimate challenge for future research. Furthermore, the

                                                     31

[[PAGE 33]]
671   correlation developed for potato may not be applicable for other food materials due to their

672   diverse nature and properties; therefore further investigation of the modulus of elasticity for

673   specific food materials is crucial.

674          In addition, the value of Poisson’s ratio was assumed for most of the cases without

675   proper justification although these values are strongly dependent on moisture content and

676   temperature, and changes throughout the drying process. The use of an inappropriate Poisson’s

677   ratio may lead to erroneous prediction of deformation. Therefore, consideration of accurate

678   Poisson’s ratio for different fruits and vegetables is important for better prediction of material

679   shrinkage during drying.

680   8. Shrinkage measuring techniques

681           Shrinkage of food sample during drying can be measured by the liquid displacement

682   method and imaging technique. The liquid displacement method gives the homogeneous

683   volume shrinkage whereas heterogeneous shrinkage (both area and volume change) can be

684   measured by imaging technique. Imaging technique for measuring shrinkage is also known as

685   non-intrusive technique. In literature several techniques were used for the shrinkage

686   measurement which are summarized below.

687          Volume of the samples can be measured by the liquid displacement method (Zogzas et

688   al., 1994; Krokida et al., 1997; Gulati et al., 2016). The experimental apparatus consists of a

689   compartment, in which the sample is put, and of a measuring burette that is marked in volume

690   scale in (ml) to measure the displaced volume of the liquid. The compartment of the apparatus

691   can be closed hermetically by a lid. The apparatus is filled with a suitable portion of a liquid.

692   Toluene, n-heptane, and mercury can be used as test liquid. The displaced volume of the test

693   liquid is measured by turning the apparatus upside down without and with the sample

                                                     32

[[PAGE 34]]
694   immersed. The measuring accuracy depends on the accuracy of the burette, usually an accuracy

695   of 0.05 ml is used.

696          Shrinkage measurement by using imaging technique involves image segmentation,

697   noise reduction and then conversion to a binary image. In the segmentation process, the

698   information of every pixel of the image is specified and noise is eliminated from the image,

699   which is then followed by converting to a binary image for identifying the surface area. Raw

700   images can be taken by X-ray micro-tomography (µCT), Laser Scanning, Scanning Electron

701   Microscope (SEM), camcorder, digital video camera attached with stereo-microscope,

702   computer vision, and colour line-scan digital camera. Raw images can be processed by different

703   software to get the shrinkage data. ImageJ, MATLAB, CAD software, UTHSCSA Image Tool,

704   Presitt algorithm, and Digital Image Correlation (DIC) algorithm are widely used for image

705   processing techniques to measure shrinkage.

706          Gulati et al. (2016) measured shrinkage during microwave drying by placing a digital

707   camcorder near the hole on the side face of the microwave and analyzing the images using

708   ImageJ software to get the area shrinkage. Hansson, Couceiro, and Fjellner (2016)

709   experimentally measured radial and tangential shrinkage of wood by tomography (CT) images

710   and compared the results with those obtained by computer-aided design (CAD) software on

711   the same images. Computer vision systems which include sample distributing unit, image

712   capture unit and image processing unit were successfully designed and implemented for

713   characterizing the drying shrinkage of tobacco lamina (Zhu, Wang, Xu, & Du, 2014). A stereo-

714   correlation technique was used to determine the volume (both the radial and axial shrinkage)

715   of a banana during convective drying where two cameras were used to determine the shrinkage

716   parameters of each camera (Madiouli et al., 2011). Recently an image processing algorithm has

717   been effectively used for measuring the relative area reduction during convective drying of

                                                    33

[[PAGE 35]]
718   sugar kelp (Sappati et al., 2017). They have developed a new image processing algorithm based

719   on pixel thresholding in MATLAB.

720   9. Conclusion

721      Drying of fruits and vegetables are very complex process where heat and mass transfer

722   occurs simultaneously along with volume reduction. Physical quality of dried food materials

723   largely depends on the extent of deformation during drying. In contrast, rehydration capacity

724   and rehydration rate of the dried fruits and vegetables are strongly related to the shrinkage.

725   These attributes can be improved by controlling the extensive deformation of the dehydrated

726   product during simultaneous heat and mass transfer.

727      This article presented the different aspects of shrinkage and the factors that affect the large

728   deformation during drying. It has been manifested from the previous literature that the fruits

729   and vegetables can be considered as elastic, hyperelastic, elastoplastic or viscoelastic while

730   developing a shrinkage model. Then the formulation of current shrinkage models and their

731   typical results have been discussed. Finally, the current challenges to develop theoretical

732   shrinkage model have been identified and discussed. The critical review in this paper identified

733   many limitations of the existing models and therefore a generalized shrinkage model that is

734   applicable for diverse fruits and vegetables is essential. This review also discussed that varying

735   drying parameters and changing mechanical properties are primarily responsible for large and

736   anisotropic shrinkage that occurs during drying. The variation of drying air velocity and

737   temperature does affect the material shrinkage. Therefore, the future research should be

738   devoted towards developing a generalized theoretical shrinkage model considering the material

739   characterization and real-time thermo-physical properties of fruits and vegetables during

740   drying. This paper will contribute to a better understanding of shrinkage phenomena and

741   associated factors through the development of a physics-based shrinkage model.
